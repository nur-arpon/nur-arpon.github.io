Story

It started with one keyboard.

Mine. Here is how a script for my own spacebar turned into SpaceZ, told with the dates to prove it.

In May 2026 I wanted one small thing: to open an app without hunting for it. I wrote an AutoHotkey script for my own keyboard, and two friends tested it with me. Hold Space, tap the first letter of an app, and it opens. Tap it again and it hides. We called it SpaceToggle OS and put it on GitHub on 26 May 2026.

It was only meant for me. But I used it every day, and every day I found something to make better, so I kept going.

From a script to a real app

In July I rebuilt the idea as a native Windows app in Rust and Tauri, with a settings window a person could actually use. In August it got its name, Spaceadom, and its first public release. After that it shipped roughly once a day: a ring of app icons under the mouse, touchpad edges, themes, builds for ARM64 laptops. In September it went to the Microsoft Store, published as SpaceZ.

Then everything else

Once one app was out in the world, the next ones came faster. FileZ, which keeps a Downloads folder tidy by itself. Spike, a desktop companion with a face and a memory, and Desk Buddy, the robot dog he is going to live in, designed part by part in CAD. Mistri Lagbe, an Android app for booking local tradespeople. And Hashi Khushi, a website for my mother's business, the first site I built for someone else.

How I learned, and how I build

I learn by shipping. I don't wait until I know everything; I start, hit a wall, read, fix it and release again. Real people using the apps find things I would never find on my own laptop, so their reports come first. And every bug gets written down, with what caused it and how it was fixed, so the same mistake doesn't happen twice.

That is how SpaceZ works for clients too: a clear plan, small steps you can see, honest status, and nothing called finished until it works on a real machine.

How I build

Four habits behind every release.

None of these are rules from a textbook. Each one came from something that went wrong first.

01

Learn by shipping

Start, hit the wall, fix it, release. A small release today teaches more than a perfect plan next month.

02

Real users first

A report from someone using the app beats any guess. The fix starts with their log, not my theory.

03

Write every bug down

What broke, why, and how it was fixed, kept in a journal so the same mistake never ships twice.

04

Say where it stands

Live means live. In development means in development. No feature is listed until it really works.
