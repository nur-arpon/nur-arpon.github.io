Journey

It started with one keyboard.

Mine. Here is how a script for my own spacebar turned into three apps on the Microsoft Store and a robot dog, told with the dates to prove it.

In May 2026 I wanted one small thing: to open an app without hunting for it. I wrote an AutoHotkey script for my own keyboard, and two friends tested it with me. Hold Space, tap the first letter of an app, and it opens. Tap it again and it hides. We called it SpaceToggle OS and put it on GitHub on 26 May 2026.

It was only meant for me. But I used it every day, and every day I found something to make better, so I kept going.

From a script to a real app

In July I rebuilt the idea as a native Windows app in Rust and Tauri, with a settings window a person could actually use. In August it got its name, Spaceadom, and its first public release. After that it shipped roughly once a day: a ring of app icons under the mouse, touchpad edges, themes, builds for ARM64 laptops. In September it went to the Microsoft Store, published as SpaceZ. Since then it has reached macOS and Linux as betas.

Then the next ones

Once one app was out in the world, the next ones came faster. FileZ keeps a Downloads folder tidy by itself, and every move it makes has a Put back button. Spike is a desktop companion with a face, a memory and a personality, and Desk Buddy is the robot dog he is going to live in, designed part by part in SolidWorks, driven from Python scripts, and 3D-printed.

Spike is where software met hardware for me: ESP32-S3 boards, a touch-screen face, servos, sensors and a battery system, all of which have to fit inside a body that looks like a finished product, not a prototype.

How I learn

I learn by shipping. I don't wait until I know everything; I start, hit a wall, read, fix it and release again. Real people using the apps find things I would never find on my own laptop, so their reports come first. And every bug gets written down, with what caused it and how it was fixed, so the same mistake doesn't happen twice.
